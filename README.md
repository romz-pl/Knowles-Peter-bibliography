# Professor Peter Knowles


**Professor Peter J. Knowles** is a theoretical and computational chemist who spent most of his career at Cardiff University. He is now an Emeritus Professor of Theoretical Chemistry, so he has moved from active full-time duties to an emeritus role, though his research output continues.

**Career**

He completed his PhD at Cambridge, then did postdoctoral work at Cambridge and at the University of Western Ontario (1985–86). He held an SERC Advanced Research Fellowship at Cambridge (1987–89), was a lecturer in chemistry at the University of Sussex (1989–95), and was Professor of Theoretical Chemistry at the University of Birmingham (1995–2004). He joined Cardiff in 2004 as Professor of Theoretical Chemistry. He also served as Head of the School of Chemistry from 2009 to 2013, and was elected a Fellow of the Learned Society of Wales in 2011.

**Research**

His work concerns new methods, approximations, and algorithms in computational molecular electronic structure theory. In practical terms, this means developing the mathematical and computational tools that predict how electrons behave in molecules, which underpins much of modern chemistry and materials science. Several areas stand out:

- **Full configuration interaction (FCI):** He is known for work on FCI methods, which are considered the exact solution within a given basis set and are used as benchmarks for testing other approximate methods. This includes studies of perturbation theory.
- **Multiconfigurational methods:** His group has contributed to multiconfigurational self-consistent field (MCSCF) and multireference configuration interaction methods, which are needed for molecules where a single electronic configuration is not a good description, such as those with bond breaking or excited states.
- **Current themes:** His recent interests include non-adiabatic effects in photochemistry, where electronic and nuclear motion are strongly coupled, and the construction of conceptual models for chemical reactions.

**Molpro**

His most widely recognised contribution is Molpro, a quantum chemistry software package developed with Hans-Joachim Werner at the University of Stuttgart. Molpro is designed for highly accurate ab initio calculations and is known for its strengths in electron correlation methods, including coupled-cluster and multireference approaches. Its emphasis on accuracy and on local correlation methods, which keep computational costs manageable for larger molecules, has made it a standard tool in academic computational chemistry. The 2012 review article describing the program, co-authored with Werner, Gerald Knizia, Frederick Manby, and Martin Schütz, has been widely cited. Knowles also co-developed the determinant-based FCI approach with Nicholas Handy, which is one of the foundations of his reputation.

**Recognition and public profile**

His university profile lists him as a media commentator, and he has spoken at science policy events, including a 2016 "Science and the Assembly" session in Cardiff.
